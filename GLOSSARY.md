# Glossary of Key Terms

These are the working definitions used by the Austin Opportunity Index project,
as presented in the Literature and Practice Review.

## Access / Accessibility

The ease with which people can reach the places, services, and activities they
need or value, given available transportation options, travel times, costs,
safety conditions, physical abilities, and the spatial distribution of
destinations. In this report, accessibility is used in the
transportation-planning sense and is distinct from, though related to,
accessibility under the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990).

## Access to opportunity

A measure of whether residents can realistically reach opportunities such as
jobs, schools, health care, grocery stores, parks, child care, social services,
and community resources. A nearby resource is not necessarily accessible if
residents cannot reach it safely, reliably, affordably, or conveniently.

## Affordable housing

Housing that is affordable to households at specified income levels, typically
through rent restrictions, income restrictions, subsidy, public ownership,
nonprofit ownership, deed restrictions, community land trust models, or other
affordability mechanisms. For analytic purposes, affordability is often
assessed relative to household income, housing costs, and income thresholds
such as Area Median Income.

## Affirmatively Furthering Fair Housing (AFFH)

The obligation of federal agencies and recipients of federal housing funds to
take proactive steps to advance the purposes of the Fair Housing Act (1968),
including addressing patterns of segregation and unequal access to housing
opportunity. HUD describes the Fair Housing Act as both prohibiting
discrimination and requiring federal agencies and recipients of federal
housing funds to affirmatively further progress toward fair housing goals.

## Aggregation

The process of combining multiple indicators into a domain score, index score,
ranking, category, or typology. Aggregation choices matter because they
determine whether strong performance in one area can offset weak performance in
another.

## Analytic geography / spatial unit

The geographic unit at which data are measured and reported, such as census
blocks, block groups, census tracts, parcels, school attendance zones,
neighborhoods, or custom grid cells. Results can vary depending on the unit
selected, so the choice of geography should match the policy decision the tool
is meant to inform.

## Conceptual orientation

Conceptual orientation refers to the underlying perspective through which an
opportunity index defines and measures opportunity. It determines whether
opportunity is understood primarily as a characteristic of places
(place-based), populations (population-based), accessibility between people
and destinations (access-based), or a combination of these perspectives.
Identifying the conceptual orientation of an index helps clarify what is being
measured and how the resulting scores should be interpreted.

## Cluster analysis

A statistical method that groups observations, such as neighborhoods or census
tracts, based on similarities across multiple indicators. In this project,
clustering is useful because it can identify distinct neighborhood types
rather than forcing all places into a single high-to-low opportunity ranking.

## Cost burden

A housing affordability condition in which a household spends more than a
specified share of income on housing costs (typically rent and utilities for
renters and mortgage, utilities, insurance and taxes for homeowners). The
common federal benchmark defines households spending more than 30% of income
on housing as cost-burdened and those spending more than 50% as severely
cost-burdened.

## Displacement

The process by which residents are forced or pressured to leave their homes or
neighborhoods because of rising housing costs, redevelopment, eviction, poor
housing conditions, loss of social supports, discrimination, environmental
change, or other direct or indirect pressures.

## Environmental burden / environmental risk

Exposure to environmental harms or hazards, including flooding, extreme heat,
air pollution, contaminated sites, industrial uses, traffic-related emissions,
wildfire risk, or other conditions that can threaten health, safety, or housing
stability.

## Fair housing

A legal and policy framework aimed at ensuring equal access to housing and
housing-related services without discrimination. Under the federal Fair
Housing Act, protected classes of residents are defined by their race, color,
national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability.

## Gentrification

A process of neighborhood change involving reinvestment, rising property
values or rents, demographic change, and shifting commercial or cultural
conditions, often in historically disinvested neighborhoods. Gentrification
can bring new amenities and investment but can also contribute to displacement
and loss of community control.

## High-opportunity / high-resource area

A place that scores well on selected measures of opportunity or resources.
This term should be interpreted carefully: a high-opportunity area may offer
strong access to schools, jobs, parks, or services, but may still be
exclusionary, unaffordable, inaccessible by transit, or at risk of
displacement.

## Indicator

A measurable variable used to represent one aspect of opportunity,
vulnerability, need, or neighborhood condition. Examples include transit travel
time to jobs, poverty rate, school quality, flood exposure, tree canopy,
housing cost burden, or distance to a grocery store. Multiple indicators are
typically combined into a final opportunity index.

## Index or composite index

A single measure created by combining multiple indicators. Composite indices
can make complex information easier to communicate, but they can also obscure
which underlying conditions are driving a score.

## Mobility-based interventions

Mobility-based interventions are housing policies that promote residential
mobility by helping households relocate to higher-opportunity neighborhoods,
thereby expanding access to educational, economic, and social opportunities.

## Normalization / standardization

A method for converting indicators measured in different units into a common
scale so they can be compared or combined. Common approaches include z-scores,
percentile ranks, and min-max scaling.

## Opportunity

The set of place-based resources, reachable destinations, social conditions,
environmental protections, institutional supports, and freedoms that help
residents live healthy, stable, connected, and self-determined lives. In this
project, opportunity is not treated as a single fixed property of a
neighborhood, but as multidimensional and dependent on household needs.

## Opportunity mapping

The use of spatial data to understand how resources, risks, and access vary
across a city or region. Opportunity maps can inform affordable housing siting,
preservation, infrastructure investment, mobility programs, anti-displacement
strategies, and cross-departmental planning.

## Target population / population focus

The group whose needs, outcomes, or access conditions an index is designed to
illuminate. Some indices are universal; others focus on children, voucher
holders, older adults, residents with disabilities, low-income households, or
communities at risk of displacement.

## Typology

A classification system that groups places into meaningful categories based on
shared characteristics. Unlike a single index score, a typology can show that
different neighborhoods face different combinations of assets, risks, and
needs, and therefore may require different policy responses.

## Vulnerability / social vulnerability

The degree to which people or places may be more likely to experience harm from
economic stress, environmental hazards, displacement pressure, health risks,
or limited access to resources. Vulnerability is shaped by both household
conditions and structural factors such as policy, infrastructure, markets, and
discrimination.
